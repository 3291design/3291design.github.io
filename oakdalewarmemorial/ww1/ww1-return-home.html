Chapter IV
The Return Home
The Armistice did not bring an immediate end to the suffering; many soldiers returned home bearing the physical toll of war.
While the history books close the Great War on the eleventh of November, the reality for the mining communities of Gwent was far more protracted. The war did not end with a sudden, joyous return to normalcy; it bled slowly into peacetime, carried home in the bodies and minds of the survivors.[1]

In this final chapter of our stories, we examine the physical and psychological burdens of service that followed men home, extending the village's mourning long after the official cessation of hostilities.
1916 – 1919 The Long Shadow of Peace
When the armistice finally took effect on 11 November 1918, church bells rang out across the Gwent valleys and colliery towns celebrated the end of four grueling years of warfare. Yet, for thousands of servicemen, leaving the uniform behind was only the beginning of a complex and often painful transition. Demobilisation was slow, chaotic, and heavily bottlenecked across channel ports and transit camps as millions of men awaited repatriation.[2]
For the families welcoming their sons, husbands, and fathers back to Oakdale, the homecoming was frequently met with a sobering reality. Many men returned carrying severe physical disabilities, chronic respiratory damage from poison gas attacks, or unhealed wounds that would shorten their lives in the months and years following the peace. Military and auxiliary medical facilities across Britain remained overwhelmed with wounded and chronically ill veterans.[3]
The tragedy of the conflict stretched far beyond active combat zones, claiming local men through accidents on home duty, post-war disease, medical discharges, and lingering wounds. These included Rifleman Albert Young, who survived the bitter initial campaigns of 1915 on the Western Front only to be tragically killed in an accident while on home service on 31 March 1916; Pte. Samuel Fisher, who endured the harsh conditions of Gallipoli before returning home wounded, only to succumb to pneumonia on 25 July 1917 at age 44; Pte. Frederick George Goodman, who was honorably discharged due to service impairment in January 1919 and awarded the Silver War Badge before ultimately succumbing to his wounds; Seaman Ivor James Nelmes, who was invalided out of the Royal Navy with advanced tuberculosis in August 1917 and passed away at home at the end of 1918; and Pte. Albert Edward Dyer, who survived four years of frontline service with the Yorkshire Light Infantry and Labour Corps before dying of his wounds in an auxiliary hospital in Birkenhead on 6 April 1919. For each of these men, the final sacrifice was extracted long after the firing had ceased.
