William Ward Davies was born in 1920, the second son of William John and Agnes Hamilton Davies. His family lived and worked at Kincoed Farm, Oakdale, where his father operated a local dairy farming business.

As the Luftwaffe began launching intense bombing campaigns against British industrial centres, cities, and suburbs, thousands of unexploded or delayed-action munitions littered civilian areas. Sapper Davies volunteered for the exceptionally perilous duties of the 67/69 Bomb Disposal Company, a specialised unit of the Royal Engineers formed to confront this unprecedented threat.
The assignment required absolute precision, steady nerves, and the willingness to work directly with highly unstable explosive ordnance designed specifically to trap and kill recovery teams. On the afternoon of Sunday, 25 August 1940, Sapper Davies' unit was dispatched to a suburban residential area north of Birmingham. Three heavy, delayed-action German bombs had deeply embedded themselves at the rear of a home at 117 Welwyndale Road, Sutton Coldfield.
As the recovery team worked meticulously to defuse and neutralise the threat, the delayed-action fuses reached their detonation parameters. The resulting catastrophic explosion ripped through the residential area, instantly killing six nearby civilians. Seven members of the Royal Engineers bomb disposal unit were caught in the direct radius of the detonation. Wartime records from the Grangetown War Project confirm that Sapper Davies survived the initial blast but was severely injured, tragically dying of his wounds at a nearby medical facility later that same day.
Following his death, Sapper Davies was brought home and laid to rest in Jerusalem Chapelyard, Woodfieldside, Wales, where his grave is meticulously cared for by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (view listing: 2004138). His name remains proudly honoured within the local history of Oakdale, keeping his memory alive for the generations that follow on the Oakdale War Memorial.
