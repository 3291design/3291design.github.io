George Rowland Williams entered military service in the Royal Army Service Corps (RASC) under service number T/247912, where the "T" prefix designated his specialised role as a Driver. He was attached to the 196th Field Ambulance, Royal Army Medical Corps (RAMC)—a mobile front-line medical unit responsible for collecting, treating, and evacuating casualties from the immediate combat zone.

In autumn 1941, as threats escalated in the Pacific, George’s unit was selected for Far East reinforcements. Embarking in October 1941 on an immense, circuitous maritime voyage to evade Axis submarines, his troopship route spanned the globe: crossing the North Atlantic to Halifax, Nova Scotia, south to Trinidad, across to Cape Town, onward to Bombay (Mumbai), and finally down through the Malacca Straits.
Driver Williams stepped ashore in Singapore on 29 January 1942, just as Japanese forces pressed down the Malayan Peninsula. Barely two weeks later, following relentless assault, the Allied garrison surrendered on 15 February 1942. George was captured at the Fall of Singapore and processed into the brutal Japanese Prisoner of War system.
Drafted into forced labor pools in the jungles of Thailand to build the infamous Burma-Siam Death Railway, prisoners endured systematic starvation, physical abuse, tropical downpours, and rampant disease. Held at the major base and transit camp of Nong Pladuk near the railway's southern terminus, Driver Williams passed away in captivity on 22 January 1944 at the age of 29.
After the war, his remains were recovered from isolated jungle sites and reinterred in the Chungkai War Cemetery, Thailand, where his grave is meticulously registered and cared for by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (view listing: T/247912). His name remains proudly honoured within the local history of Oakdale, keeping his memory alive for the generations that follow on the Oakdale War Memorial.
