Northwest Europe Infantry
The final continental campaign demanded grueling infantry advances through Normandy, the bitter Ardennes, and into Germany.
The Liberation of Europe
Opening with the momentous amphibious landings of D-Day in June 1944, the Northwest Europe campaign was the decisive Allied thrust to break the grip of Nazi occupation on the continent.[1] The campaign required fighting through fiercely defended bocage terrain, executing massive river crossings, and enduring harsh winter conditions before finally crossing the Rhine and forcing the unconditional surrender of German forces.[1]

In this final part, we recognize the valley servicemen who fought across France, Belgium, and Holland, and we honor the lasting cost of victory for those who gave their lives during the ultimate advance into Germany.
1944 – 1946 Normandy and the Advance through Germany
Following the establishment of the Allied beachheads, infantry and armored divisions engaged in a slow, punishing war of attrition through the Norman countryside.[2] The fighting was defined by close-quarters combat in dense hedgerows, stone farmsteads, and ruined villages, eventually culminating in the entrapment of German armies in the Falaise Pocket and a rapid pursuit across France and Belgium.[2]

As the Allies reached the Siegfried Line and the borders of the Reich, German resistance stiffened dramatically.[1] Infantrymen endured frozen trenches during the Ardennes winter offensive, cleared heavily fortified urban centers, and forced hazardous assaults across major river obstacles including the Rhine and the Elbe.[2]
Even following the formal cessation of hostilities in May 1945, the severe physical toll of continuous combat continued to claim servicemen.[3] Many veterans serving in the post-war occupational forces succumbed to severe wounds, respiratory infections, or aggressive illnesses contracted during the exhausting winter campaigns, serving active military duty well into the demobilization period.[3]
The lingering cost of the final victory in Europe was felt profoundly back home. The ultimate sacrifice required to dismantle the German war machine is permanently honored through soldiers such as Albert Thomas (South Wales Borderers, transferred to 1st Bn. The Tyneside Scottish / Black Watch), who gave his life in January 1946 following the grueling infantry campaigns at the close of the war.
