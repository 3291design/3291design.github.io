Bomber Command & Operational Aircrews
Flying through night skies over occupied Europe, Oakdale’s airmen operated at the tip of the spear in the war's most perilous campaign.
1939 – 1945 The Air War Over Europe & Beyond
The air war waged by RAF Bomber Command was an unrelenting 2,074-day campaign aimed at dismantling Nazi Germany's industrial capacity, transportation infrastructure, and military strongholds. Between 1939 and 1945, Bomber Command flew over 364,000 operational sorties. For the young men manning multi-engine heavy bombers like the Avro Lancaster, Handley Page Halifax, and Vickers Wellington, every mission demanded hours of navigating pitch-black, freezing skies pierced by anti-aircraft flak and prowled by radar-equipped German night fighters.[2]
Vickers Wellington Mk Is of No. 9 Squadron in flight, 1939.
Photo credit: Imperial War Museums
The operational statistics of Bomber Command remain among the most harrowing of any British service branch. Out of approximately 125,000 aircrew members who served, 55,573 were killed—a devastating fatality rate of over 44 percent. A further 8,403 were wounded, and 9,838 became Prisoners of War. Over 8,950 combat aircraft were lost in action, turning the night skies over Western Europe into a vast battlefield where an airman's statistical chance of surviving a full 30-mission operational tour was less than 50 percent.[2]
Oakdale's airmen were distributed across three key operational units within the RAF structure:[1][2]
  • No. 9 & No. 106 Squadrons: Operating four-engined Avro Lancaster heavy bombers on strategic deep-penetration night raids over industrial Germany and Southern Europe.
  • No. 51 & No. 148 Squadrons: Flying Handley Page Halifaxes on heavy bombardment runs as well as covert "Special Duties" drops delivering arms and agents to partisan resistance forces in the Balkans.
  • No. 15 & No. 179 Squadrons: Manning Vickers Wellingtons on strategic bombing operations over Northern Europe and long-range anti-submarine hunter-killer patrols across Atlantic and Mediterranean choke points.

The tight-knit mining communities of the Sirhowy Valley felt this crushing statistical toll acutely as Air Ministry telegrams arrived in Oakdale detailing missing aircraft, mid-air airfield collisions, and bombers lost at sea. Whether operating as navigators managing complex flight calculations, rear gunners enduring sub-zero temperatures in isolated tail turrets, or flight engineers managing complex Merlin engine outputs, these young men faced an environment where a single searchlight lock or mechanical failure over enemy territory carried immediate, fatal consequences.[2]
The relentless operational toll of the air war is permanently etched into valley history through the sacrifice of six local airmen, including Kenneth Thomas John Adams, James Canniff, Frederick Moses Cyril Henry Harris, Thomas Vardre Lewis, Glyndwr Reardon, and Wyndham Royce Silcocks.
