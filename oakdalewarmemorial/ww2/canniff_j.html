James Canniff was born to James and Mary Canniff. Demonstrating an early passion for aviation and mechanical engineering, he stepped forward to join the Royal Air Force as a teenager in March 1938, well before the formal outbreak of World War II.

As heavy four-engine bombers were introduced, the RAF established the specialized role of Flight Engineer. Operating alongside the pilot, Canniff was responsible for managing the bomber's complex fuel systems, monitoring four Rolls-Royce Merlin engines, calculating mechanical output, and assisting during emergency procedures.
By the summer of 1942, Sergeant Canniff was fully operational and posted to No. 106 Squadron at RAF Coningsby in Lincolnshire. This period marked a historic transition for the squadron as it converted to the legendary Avro Lancaster Mk.I heavy bomber. Operating under the leadership of Wing Commander Guy Gibson, No. 106 Squadron was flying dangerous night operations deep into Germany.
On the night of 24–25 August 1942, Sergeant Canniff took off aboard Lancaster Mk.I (Serial Number R5684) targeting the industrial city of Frankfurt. Navigating dense anti-aircraft fire and Luftwaffe night fighters to execute their bombing run, the crippled Lancaster sustained fatal damage on the return leg. Attempting to cross the North Sea, the aircraft crashed off the Belgian coast, with no survivors.
Initially listed as missing in action, Sergeant Canniff’s body was later recovered after washing ashore near Kijkduin in the Netherlands. He was laid to rest with full military honours at The Hague (Westduin) General Cemetery, where his grave is cared for by the Commonwealth War Graves Commission (view listing: 652687). His name remains proudly honoured within the local history of Oakdale, keeping his memory alive for the generations that follow on the Oakdale War Memorial.
